    01 The word

    What is Hudoq?

    Hudoq means mask.

    It is also a dance.

    Men put on leaf cloaks and carved faces.

    Most say they play spirits who guard the rice.

    • WhoDayak Bahau, Modang
      and their kin
    • WhereEast Kalimantan,
      Borneo, Indonesia
    • WhenMostly after rice planting.
      Around Sept–Nov.

    Each village does it its own way. So where are we?

    02 The river

    Where is Hudoq from?

    1. Borneo.

      The third-largest island on Earth. Old, wide rainforest.

    2. East Kalimantan.

      One of Indonesia’s five provinces on Borneo. Its big river: the Mahakam, 980 km long.

    3. Upriver.

      Mahakam Ulu: East Kalimantan’s youngest regency, about 88% forest. Hudoq is its mascot.

    4. Downriver, too.

      In Samarinda, near the river mouth, Bahau families have held a yearly Hudoq since 2015.

    000 km

    Bahau name for the river: Mekam · Hudoq lives in West Kutai too

    03 The rice

    Why do they dance?

    SeasonPlant

    1. First, the seeds.

      Upriver, rice grows in dry hill fields cleared from forest. Farmers poke holes with sticks and drop seeds in.

    2. Then, the worry.

      Pests. Disease. A bad harvest means a hungry year.

    3. So the spirits come.

      Masked dancers play spirits sent by ancestors, or by gods. Their job: guard the rice till harvest.

      1898 A Dutch explorer saw the lead dancer wave a long carved hook — to pull stray rice souls back.

    4. Not a word.

      In 1898, masked dancers kept silent. Spirits can’t talk, people said — speaking could strike you dead.

    5. Two tellings.

      Telling A

      Guardians

      Spirits from above. Sent to protect the crop.

      Telling B

      Pests

      In some tellings, the masks are pests — thirteen, one source says. Two human hudoqs chase them off.

      Both are told. Many dances mix them: guardians, ancestors, clowning pests.

    04 The legend

    A sword. A whirlpool. A hidden kingdom.

    1. A sword.
      A whirlpool.
      A hidden kingdom.

      Where did the masks come from? One Modang & Ga’ay story · spellings vary
    2. i

      Halaeng Heboung drops his sword in the Kejin River.

    3. ii

      In a whirlpool he meets Selo Sen Yaeng — a spirit from a kingdom under the riverbed.

    4. iii

      They marry. They have a son, Buaq Selo.

    5. iv

      To please him, she calls her people to dance. Their true faces are terrifying.

    6. v

      Scared, he goes home with their son. He tells the village — and has masks carved like those faces.

    7. vi

      The dance keeps the bond alive: river people and his descendants.

    05 The faces

    In the dark,
    they watch.

    Those faces, in wood. Move your light — find all eleven. 0/11

      Eleven faces.

      Big round eyes. Wide wing ears. Long noses and beaks. Fangs.

      • 4 kinds at one 2018 festival, a reporter saw: human, dragon, bird, pig.
      • Birds rank highest, one catalogue says. Then reptiles. Then mammals.
      • White, black, red, yellow. Often said to mean purity, grandeur, courage, prosperity.

      06 The mask

      Turn it
      around.

      Pick one up · drag to spin, or just scroll

      1. Eyes

        Big, round, often shiny. In 1898, some were lids of mirror boxes or copper betel boxes.

      2. Beak

        A long nose, or a beak. Bird shapes, like the hornbill, are most common upriver.

      3. Ears

        Huge wing ears. In 1898 they were set with leopard teeth — real or carved.

      4. Jaw

        Old masks had a loose lower jaw that clacked as the dancer moved.

      5. Hollow

        Light wood, often jelutung. Hollow behind — a face to step into. In 1898, each young man carved his own.

      07 The suit

      Dressed as
      a spirit.

      A mask is just the face. The rest is feathers, beads and leaves.

      Crown

      Long feathers. Hornbill or Argus pheasant.

      Mask

      Carved wood. Often a white face with black and red lines.

      Chest

      Beads, and a big round disc that catches the light.

      Cloak

      Usually banana leaves, split into fringe. Fresh each time. Old leaves aren’t burned — they rot back into the soil.

      10kg or so for a full costume, a 2018 report says

      08 The beat

      Circle. Stomp. Flap.

      Dressed? Then the gong starts.

      • Right foot first. To scare off pests.
      • Arms flap like wings.
      • Turn right to call in good. Turn left to shed bad luck, a local scholar explains.
      • Three laps around the village, some say — at least.
      • The gong keeps time. Stray from it, an 1898 account warns, and risk bad luck.

      Your turn.

      Try the rhythm. Tap the gong on the beat — space, click or touch. Just a game, not the ritual.

      Bahau say the dance must be lively, so the rice spirit is happy to stay in the field.

      09 Then

      Written down in the 1890s.
      Filmed later.

      No one knows how old Hudoq is. These are some early records.

      1898

      A.W. Nieuwenhuis, a Dutch doctor, sees it at Long Blu’u on the Mahakam. Mirror eyes. Clacking jaws. Leaf cloaks.

      Women, too

      He also saw a women’s masquerade: upside-down rattan baskets on their heads, a face sewn on white cloth.

      Growls, not words

      He counts 23 spirits. When the chief speaks to them, they answer with growls and nods.

      10 Now

      Still
      dancing.

      In colour now. And in crowds.

      Hudoq Pekayang

      “Pekayang” means visiting each other. Villages take turns as host — then each dances in the longhouse.

      1. Feed the mask. Sticky rice and boiled eggs.
      2. Words with the leaders — in the Hudoq language.
      3. The host dances first.
      4. Every village takes a turn.

      A short timeline

      1. 1994

        Hosting gets costly. Villages agree: guests bring their own food. The visits go on.

      2. 2013

        Indonesia lists Hudoq as national intangible heritage. (Not on UNESCO’s lists.)

      3. 2014

        The visiting festival is renamed Hudoq Pekayang.

      4. 2016

        802 dancers from 13 villages at Hudoq Pekayang.

      5. 2018

        2,230 dancers at once — a record in Indonesia’s MURI book. A Hudoq-inspired gown wins Best National Costume at Miss Supranational.

      6. 2019

        25 hours of non-stop Hudoq — another MURI record.

      7. Oct 2026

        About 260 men from 13 villages dance at Long Pahangai II. Elders also set next year’s planting.

      12 The care

      Alive.
      But fragile.

      • It needs fields. Land is short in Tering Baru, so elders planted a tiny rice plot in a monastery yard.
      • Young people drift. In 2016, a local leader said modern life was pulling Hudoq under.
      • Some parts are fading. In 2019, a customary leader called the Nebeq dance almost lost, and the song Ngaraang Hudoq rare.
      • Festivals help — and change it. Pride and income, yes. But the old sacred timing loosens.
      • Many hands help. Villages and customary councils, with local government, universities and conservation groups.

      If you watch

      It’s a ritual before it’s a show.
      Ask before you film. Credit dancers and carvers.
