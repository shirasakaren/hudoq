    01 The word

    What is Hudoq?

    Hudoq means mask.

    It is also a dance.

    Men put on leaf cloaks and carved faces.

    They play the spirits that guard the rice.

    • WhoDayak Bahau, Modang
      and their kin
    • WhereEast Kalimantan,
      Borneo, Indonesia
    • WhenAfter the rice is planted.
      Roughly Sept–Nov.

    Not the same everywhere. Each community tells it in its own way.

    02 The river

    Where is Hudoq from?

    1. Borneo.

      The third-largest island on Earth. Old, wide rainforest.

    2. East Kalimantan.

      Indonesia’s slice of Borneo. One long river runs through it: the Mahakam. 980 km.

    3. Upriver.

      Mahakam Ulu. The newest regency in the province. About 88% forest. The Hudoq heartland.

    4. Downriver, too.

      In Samarinda, Bahau families keep Hudoq going — far from the upper river.

    Bahau name for the river: Mekam

    03 The rice

    Why do they dance?

    SeasonPlant

    1. First, the seeds.

      Rice grows in dry fields cut from the forest. Farmers poke holes, drop seeds in, and cover them.

    2. Then, the worry.

      Pests. Disease. A bad harvest means a hungry year.

    3. So the spirits come.

      Masked dancers play spirits sent by ancestors, or by gods. Their job: guard the rice till harvest.

      1890s A Dutch explorer saw the lead dancer carry a long carved hook — to pull the rice’s soul home.

    4. They don’t speak.

      Spirits can’t talk. So the masked dancers keep quiet — one old account says speaking could kill them.

    5. Two tellings.

      Telling A

      Guardians

      Spirits from above. Sent to protect the crop.

      Telling B

      Pests

      The masks are the pests — thirteen of them. Two human hudoqs chase them off.

      Both are told. Neither is the only truth.

    04 The legend

    A sword.
    A whirlpool.
    A hidden kingdom.

    A Modang & Ga’ay telling · names are spelled several ways

    i

    Halaeng Heboung drops his sword in the Kejin River.

    ii

    In a whirlpool he meets Selo Sen Yaeng — a spirit from a kingdom under the riverbed.

    iii

    They marry. They have a son, Buaq Selo.

    iv

    To please him, she calls her people to dance. Their true faces are terrifying.

    v

    He gets scared and goes home. He tells the village what he saw — and carves masks to match the faces.

    vi

    The masks keep two worlds linked: water people and land people.

    05 The faces

    In the dark,
    they watch.

    Move your light. Find all eleven. 0/11

      Eleven faces.

      Big round eyes. Wide wing ears. Long noses and beaks. Fangs.

      • 4 kinds seen at one 2018 festival: human, dragon, bird, pig.
      • Bird masks rank highest. Then reptiles. Then mammals.
      • White, black, red, yellow. Often said to mean purity, grandeur, courage, prosperity.

      06 The mask

      Turn it
      around.

      Drag to spin · or just scroll

      1. Eyes

        Big and round. Often shiny. In the 1890s, carvers used the lids of mirror boxes.

      2. Beak

        A long nose, or a beak. Bird shapes are common upriver.

      3. Ears

        Huge wing ears. On old masks they carried leopard teeth.

      4. Jaw

        Hinged, so it clacked when the dancer moved.

      5. Hollow

        Cut from one block of light wood. Hollow at the back — a face to step into.

      07 The suit

      Dressed as
      a spirit.

      Crown

      Long feathers. Hornbill or Argus pheasant.

      Mask

      Carved wood. A white face with black and red lines.

      Chest

      Beads, and a big round disc that catches the light.

      Cloak

      Banana leaves, shredded into fringe. Fresh every time. Old leaves aren’t burned — they rot back into the soil.

      10kg, roughly, for a full costume

      08 The beat

      Circle. Stomp. Flap.

      • Right foot first. To scare off pests.
      • Arms flap like wings.
      • Turn right to call in good. Turn left to shake off bad luck.
      • Three laps around the village, at least.
      • The gong keeps time. Miss the beat, risk bad luck.

      Your turn.

      Keep the rice spirit happy. Tap the gong on the beat. Space, click, or touch.

      Old Bahau accounts: the dance must be lively, so the rice spirit wants to stay in the field.

      00:00● REC

      1948. “Het Land van de Hoedo” — a Dutch film. Masked dancers step out of the forest.

      1890s

      A Dutch explorer, A.W. Nieuwenhuis, describes a Bahau mask dance on the Mahakam. Mirror-lid eyes. Clacking jaws. Frayed banana-leaf cloaks.

      Women, too

      He also saw a women’s masquerade: upside-down rattan baskets on their heads, a face sewn on white cloth.

      Why film it

      Because it was fading in some places, and strong in others. It still is.

      10 Now

      Still
      dancing.

      Colour is back. So are the crowds.

      Hudoq Pekayang

      “Pekayang” means visiting each other. Villages take turns hosting, and dance for each other in a circle inside the longhouse.

      1. Feed the mask. Sticky rice and boiled eggs.
      2. Speak to the leaders — in the Hudoq language.
      3. The host dances first.
      4. Every village takes a turn.

      A short timeline

      1. 2013

        Indonesia lists Hudoq as national intangible heritage. (Not on UNESCO’s lists.)

      2. 2014

        The visiting festival is renamed Hudoq Pekayang.

      3. 2016

        802 dancers from 13 villages.

      4. 2018

        2,230 dancers in one show — a MURI record. Same year, a Hudoq costume wins at Miss Supranational.

      5. 2019

        25 hours of non-stop Hudoq.

      6. Oct 2026

        About 260 men from 13 villages dance at Long Pahangai II.

      12 The care

      Alive.
      But fragile.

      • It needs fields. In Tering Baru, farmland is scarce. Elders planted a tiny rice plot in a monastery yard — just so Hudoq could go on.
      • Young people drift. Officials say modern life is pulling the art under.
      • Some parts are almost gone. The Nebeq dance has not been seen for years. The song Ngaraang Hudoq is rarely sung.
      • Festivals help — and bend the rules. They bring pride and money. They also loosen the old timing.
      • People are on it. Villages, customary councils, local government, universities, conservation groups.
